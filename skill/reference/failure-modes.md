# The six ways a kill criterion fails

Quick reference. The first five belong to the criterion; the sixth belongs to
the claim.

## 1. Wrong signal
Measures share price rather than the business.

- Bad: "Stock falls 20%" / "Underperforms sector peers"
- Why: price and business performance are separate scoreboards that only
  sometimes agree. You can be right about the mechanism and lose money on
  multiple compression, or be wrong and make money in a sector rally.

## 2. Unobservable
No document from any publisher reports it on a schedule.

- Bad: "Management reports the electrician gap has been filled by half"
- Bad: "Management discloses no new integrated projects"
- Good: "Segment revenue growth falls below 45%" — a number in a filing table
- Test: which document, which publisher, what schedule?
- Note: the publisher need not be the company. A government agency's annual
  procurement plan counts. A competitor's 10-K counts.
- Why it is the worst mode: a missing criterion fails validation loudly. This
  one passes, looks rigorous, and silently never resolves.

## 3. Wrong level
A real reported figure, but not the one the claim is about.

- Claim about the transportation segment, criterion measures consolidated margin
- Why: a weak segment elsewhere drags the total down and kills a thesis that
  was right. False negative.

## 4. Doesn't settle it
Happens or does not, you still do not know.

- Bad: "The government awards the company Contract X during 2026"
- Test both directions. Award lands: claim dead? Maybe not — revenue may not
  arrive for years. No award: claim confirmed? No — it might be delayed.

## 5. Timeframe mismatch
The criterion's clock is shorter than the claim's.

- Claim: "a durable competitive moat lasting years"
- Criterion: "growth below 20% in the next two quarters"
- Why: two strong quarters look identical whether you have a moat or are simply
  in a hot market. Either shorten the claim or extend the horizon.

## 6. Bundled claim
Several predictions in one sentence. Belongs to the claim, not the criterion.

- "Growth slows AND margins compress AND free cash flow suffers"
- No single criterion can kill it. Split into separate theses.

---

## The mode the five cannot catch

**A fabricated fact.** An auditor can reason impeccably from a false premise —
asserting a real program does not exist, or that a company does not disclose a
figure it publishes quarterly. The logic is sound; the input is fiction.

This needs a separate pass: list every fact-about-the-world the reasoning
depends on, then check those facts. You cannot catch a hidden assumption. You
can catch a declared one.
